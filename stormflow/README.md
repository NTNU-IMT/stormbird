# Stormflow

## Purpose
Stormflow is a CFD solver specialized for actuator line simulations. That is, it is NOT intended to be a general solver, but rather dedicated to this one purpose. The hypotheses behind the development is that a simple solver may prove to a be a good fit for this type of modeling.

## Methods
### General CFD details
The solver implements a structured, fixed cell size, cartesian grid and solves the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations using fourth order finite difference schemes. The flow variables are stored using a staggered approach; the pressure lives at the cell center, while the velocity components live on the positive faces of each cell (u on the positive x-face, v on the positive y-face, and w on the positive z-face). Time stepping is done explicitly. The pressure projection step is solved using a geometric multigrid method. The boundaries on the outer grid boundary are enforces through ghost cell, except for the pressure, where the boundaries are also enforces in the kernels directly (to simplify the GPU execution, see the section on Software architecture for more)

The outer boundaries of the domain are defined relative to the up direction of the wind environment, which must be aligned with one of the grid axes. The face at the bottom of the domain is the ground, which is a slip wall. The face at the top is open, with a zero gradient condition on the velocity and a zero pressure. All the remaining faces are inlet/outlet boundaries, where the velocity is set to the inflow velocity (the apparent wind, as a function of height) where the flow enters the domain, and has a zero gradient where it leaves it. These faces have a zero gradient condition on the pressure. The `slip_wall_boundary_override` setting can turn any face into a slip wall, except the top, as it is the only face where the pressure is fixed. Slip walls have a zero gradient condition on the pressure.

### Actuator line method
The actuator line model is based on the Stormbird library. See the [Stormbird folder](../stormbird) for implementation details. In very short terms, the actuator line model computes sectional forces on lifting surfaces, which are projected back to the CFD solver through body forces. 

### Geometry
Other geometries can also be included in the simulation, but then only through deliberately simplified approaches. The goal is not necessarily to predict accurate flow over surfaces in general, but rather capture the overall effect of those geometries on the actuator line model. That is, some simplification on the, e.g., boundary layer and exact details of the flow around the geometry is seen as perfectly fine, as long as the overall effect of that geometry on the flow field is still captured. More specifically, no slip boundaries are implemented using the data immersion technique, while slip boundaries are implemented using a symmetry conditions and conventional immersed boundary ghost cell techniques. The ghost cell (mirror) correction only reaches a few cells into the geometries, so the velocity further inside them is set to zero with the data immersion. The same is done for the cells where the mirrored image point is inside a geometry, or outside or less than one cell from the boundary of the domain, for instance just inside the bottom of a geometry that stands on, or less than a cell above, the ground. The interpolation of the image point would otherwise read the ghost cells below the ground, which mirror the same cells back to themselves. Without this, the flow inside the geometries is unconstrained, and can grow without bounds. 

By default, the wall boundaries are only enforced on the velocity field directly, meaning that the walls only affect the pressure through the change they cause in the velocity field. Optionally, a zero-gradient (Neumann) boundary condition can also be enforced on the pressure at the walls. This is done by blending the pressure in the cells just inside each geometry towards the pressure at their mirror image point across the wall surface, in every Jacobi iteration of the multigrid solver. The condition is controlled independently of how the walls are represented in the velocity solver, through the `zero_gradient_on_walls` setting of the pressure solver, which can be `NotUsed` (default), `SlipWallsOnly`, `NoSlipWallsOnly` or `AllWalls`. The interpolation order used to sample the pressure at the mirror image points is set by `zero_gradient_interpolation_order` (`Tricubic`, the default, or `Trilinear`). The condition is still experimental, and works on both the CPU and the GPU version of the pressure solver. The geometries should be at least a few cells thick for it to be well defined.

### Wall model for no-slip walls
By default, the no-slip walls use the data immersion, which blends the velocity towards zero in a band of a few cells around the surface. This is robust, also for thin geometries, but the resulting wall shear stress depends on the grid resolution rather than on the physics of the boundary layer. As an alternative, the setting `"no_slip_wall_treatment": "WallModel"` replaces the data immersion with a wall model, which is the momentum counterpart of the wall functions in the turbulence model:

1. Inside the no-slip geometries, the velocity is set with the same mirror (ghost cell) correction as the slip walls. The flow therefore does not go through the surface, but slips along it, and the velocity just outside the surface is not damped.
2. The wall shear stress from the log-law, `tau_w = u_tau^2`, is added as a momentum sink in a band of two cells on the fluid side of the surface. The friction velocity is computed from the tangential velocity at a reference point 2.5 cells from the wall, with the standard log-law (`kappa = 0.41`, `E = 9.8`), or the linear profile of the viscous sublayer when the reference point is inside it. The sink is distributed over the band with a smoothed delta function (a hat function) of the wall distance, so that its integral across the band is the wall shear stress per unit wall area. Only the components tangential to the wall are affected.
3. Deep inside the geometries, and where the mirror image point is outside the domain or inside a geometry, the velocity is set to zero with the data immersion, in the same way as for the slip geometries.

The sink is applied point-implicitly to the predicted velocity, as `-tau_w delta u / |U_t|`, where `delta` is the delta function and `U_t` the tangential velocity at the reference point. This equals the full wall shear stress when the local velocity is parallel to, and as large as, the reference velocity. The implicit form is unconditionally stable, and can never reverse the flow, so the wall model does not limit the time step. The log-law uses `effective_viscosity`, which should therefore be the molecular viscosity. The wall model works both with and without the turbulence model, and on both the CPU and the GPU.

This is different from the typical approaches. In a conventional, body fitted, RANS solver, such as OpenFOAM, the first cell center above the wall is placed in the log-law region, and the wall function sets the eddy viscosity at the wall face (`nutkWallFunction`), so that the diffusive flux through the wall face equals the log-law wall shear stress. The immersed boundary wall models in the literature (e.g., Tessicini et al., 2002; Roman et al., 2009) instead reconstruct the near-wall velocity: the velocity in the cells closest to the wall is set from a log-law profile, based on the velocity at an image point further out. Neither fits well here. There are no wall faces on the cartesian grid where a wall flux can be imposed, and forcing a log-law profile across one or two cells creates large, unresolved velocity gradients in exactly the cells where the 4th order stencils and the mirror correction are the least accurate, which both disturbs the convection and creates unphysical production of turbulence. The approach here imposes what matters for the overall effect of the geometry on the flow: the total momentum loss to the wall, which is the integral of the wall shear stress over the surface, independent of the grid resolution. The details of the velocity profile inside the boundary layer are not resolved, which is consistent with the purpose of the solver. As a side effect, the implementation mainly reuses the existing slip-wall mirror correction, and only adds a small kernel for the wall shear stress.

The wall model requires geometries that are at least a few cells thick, like the zero-gradient condition on the pressure, as the mirror correction is not well defined for thinner geometries. The faces where the mirror image ends up inside a geometry are set to zero, but very thin parts will still be treated less accurately than with the data immersion. The time step is often smaller than with the data immersion. As the flow slips along the surfaces, it accelerates around sharp convex edges, in the same way as in an inviscid flow, while the data immersion damps the velocity close to the surface. The wall shear stress is too small to change this. For the SOBC1 superstructure, which has many sharp edges, the largest velocity close to the edges was about 2.5 times the apparent wind speed, compared to about 1.7 times with the data immersion, which gave a time step about 40% smaller. The mirrored velocity in the cells just inside the surfaces is also included when the time step is computed, but is about as large as the velocity just outside.

### Turbulence model
Optionally, a RANS turbulence model can be used. The main purpose is to allow for more direct comparisons with conventional RANS solvers, such as OpenFOAM, where the eddy viscosity has a large effect on the wakes, also far away from any geometry. Currently, the realizable k-epsilon model is implemented, in the same way as `realizableKE` in OpenFOAM. The model is turned on with the `turbulence` field in the simulation setup (other fields omitted):

```json
{
  "effective_viscosity": 1.5e-5,
  "turbulence": {
    "model": {"RealizableKEpsilon": {}},
    "inlet": {
      "AtmosphericBoundaryLayer": {"friction_velocity": 0.5, "roughness_length": 0.01}
    }
  }
}
```

When a turbulence model is used, `effective_viscosity` is the molecular viscosity. The model coefficients can be changed in the model object (`a0`, `c2`, `sigma_k` and `sigma_epsilon`, with the same defaults as in OpenFOAM). The turbulence at the inlet is specified through k and epsilon, with one of the following options: `IntensityAndLengthScale`, `IntensityAndViscosityRatio` (default, with an intensity of 1% and a viscosity ratio of 10), `AtmosphericBoundaryLayer`, which is the equilibrium profile used by OpenFOAM's `atmBoundaryLayerInlet` conditions, with the height measured from the water plane of the wind environment, or `Fixed`, which uses the same given values of k and epsilon at all heights. The remaining settings are `wall_treatment` (`WallFunction`, default, or `ZeroGradient`), `convection_scheme` (`LimitedLinear`, default, which uses the same limiter as OpenFOAM's `limitedLinear 1`, or `Upwind`), `nr_jacobi_iterations` (default 2) and `max_eddy_viscosity` (default 1e5, as a safeguard only).

The turbulent stresses are added to the momentum equation with a 2nd order stencil, where the diagonal part is treated implicitly. The inner iterations of each time step therefore act as Jacobi iterations on the implicit diffusion. This makes the diffusion unconditionally stable, so a large eddy viscosity does not limit the time step, which would otherwise be the case with explicit time stepping. The transport equations for the turbulence fields are solved once per time step, after the velocity and the pressure, in the same implicit way, with the time derivative, the upwind part of the convection, the diffusion and the sink terms on the diagonal, and a few Jacobi iterations. The inlet/outlet faces of the velocity are inlet/outlet faces for the turbulence fields, while the remaining faces have a zero gradient condition.

The geometries are handled in the same simplified way as for the velocity. Inside all geometries, the turbulence fields are set to the values at the mirrored image point across the surface, which gives a zero gradient condition on all walls. For the no-slip walls, standard wall functions (the same as OpenFOAM's `epsilonWallFunction` and `nutkWallFunction`) set the production of k and the value of epsilon in the fluid cells within two cells of the surface, which is where the velocity is affected by the no-slip correction with the data immersion, and where the wall shear stress is applied with the wall model. The wall distance is taken from the signed distance function, and the wall shear is computed from the tangential velocity just outside that region. The goal is not accurate boundary layers, but to avoid the unphysical production of turbulence that the large velocity gradients created by the no-slip correction would otherwise cause, so that the turbulence in the separated flow and the wakes behaves similarly to a conventional RANS solver. The eddy viscosity is blended to zero inside the no-slip geometries, with the same blending function as the no-slip velocity correction.

The exported fields include the transported turbulence fields (`k` and `epsilon`) and the eddy viscosity used by the velocity solver. Note that, as in OpenFOAM, the isotropic part of the turbulent stress is included in the pressure.

## Software architecture
The code is deliberately simple, and implements only the necessary functionalities for the purpose of this solver. At the same time, due to the fact that a fixed cell size grid requires many cells - relative to an unstructured grid or structured grid with varying spatial resolution - computational speed is also seen as very important. The main computational bottle neck for this type of simulation is likely the data transfer from the memory to whatever cores executes the code. The different parts of the solver is therefore implemented in as various kernels with a stencil approach for the logic to minimize data transfer. The kernels are generally executed in parallel. On the CPU, the kernels are executed using the rayon crate. The same kernels also exist as GPU kernels written in wgsl, which are executed using the wgpu crate.

### CPU and GPU execution
The solver consists of two main parts: a velocity solver, which updates the velocity field (convection, diffusion, body forces, geometry corrections and boundary conditions), and a pressure solver, which solves the pressure projection step with the multigrid method. Both parts can be executed either on the CPU or on the GPU, and the choice is made independently for each of them. That is, all four combinations are possible. Running both on the GPU is the fastest option, but it is also possible to, for instance, run only the pressure solver on the GPU on a machine where the GPU memory is too limited to hold the entire solver. The velocity solver stores more fields than the pressure solver, and therefore needs roughly three times as much memory.

The turbulence model, when used, is a third part, which is kept as separate from the others as possible. It only reads the velocity, and writes the eddy viscosity, which the velocity solver uses for the turbulent stresses. Without a turbulence model, the velocity solver is unchanged. The turbulence solver always runs on the same platform as the velocity solver, and on the GPU, the two share the velocity and eddy viscosity buffers directly, so no data is transferred between them. The model independent parts of the turbulence solver (the boundary conditions, the wall treatment, the transport discretization and the order of the steps) are shared by all models, so adding a new model mainly consists of adding its coefficients and kernels, see `turbulence_solver/models/mod.rs`.

The platforms are selected in the simulation setup file. The velocity solver is controlled by the `velocity_solver_compute_platform` field, while the pressure solver is controlled by the `compute_platform` field in the multigrid settings. Both default to `CPU`. A setup that runs everything on the GPU looks like this (other fields omitted):

```json
{
  "velocity_solver_compute_platform": "GPU",
  "pressure_solver": {
    "Multigrid": {
      "compute_platform": "GPU"
    }
  }
}
```

The data exchange between the CPU and the GPU is kept to a minimum:

- When both solvers run on the GPU, they share the same GPU device and buffers. The right hand side of the pressure equation is computed directly into the pressure solver's buffer, and the pressure is read directly from the pressure solver's result, so no data is transferred between the CPU and the GPU during the pressure projection. When the two solvers run on different platforms, the right hand side and the pressure are transferred for every pressure solve.
- The actuator line model always runs on the CPU, as it depends on data structures and functionality in the Stormbird library. When the velocity solver runs on the GPU, only the velocity in the cells close to the actuator lines is transferred to the CPU, and only the resulting body forces in the same cells are transferred back.
- The maximum velocity, which is used to compute a time step from a Courant number, is computed on the GPU, so only a single value is transferred for each time step.
- The full fields are only transferred to the CPU when they are explicitly requested, for instance when exporting the results.

All precomputed data, such as the inlet velocity profile, the geometry corrections and the actuator line cells, is computed on the CPU when the simulation is built, and used directly by the CPU version and uploaded once by the GPU version. The CPU and GPU versions therefore implement the same algorithms, and give the same results within floating point accuracy.

### Coarsest level of the multigrid solver
The coarsest level of the multigrid method can either be solved approximately with additional Jacobi iterations (`"coarsest_level_solver": "Jacobi"`, the default), or exactly with Gaussian elimination on a dense matrix (`"coarsest_level_solver": "Exact"`). The exact solver requires the dense matrix to be stored, and is executed on the CPU also for the GPU version of the pressure solver. It can therefore be very slow, and use a lot of memory, if the grid cannot be coarsened much, which is why it is not the default.

## Known TODOs
### Actuator line projection for moving wings
The cells that interact with the actuator line, together with their projection weights, are computed once, when the simulation is built, based on the initial position and orientation of the wings. They are not updated if the wings move or rotate during the simulation, for instance if a controller changes the local wing angles (`update_controller` returns whether such an update is needed, but this is currently ignored by the solver). This is fine for the current use, which is limited to steady wings, and also for wings that only rotate around their span line as long as the projection function is symmetric around it (`chord_factor` equal to `thickness_factor`). However, for moving wings in general, or for rotating wings with a non-symmetric projection function, the forces will be projected based on the initial geometry. To support this, the projection data should be recomputed whenever the geometry of the actuator line changes.

### Improvements to the wall model
The wall model for the no-slip walls is a first version, and a few things could be improved:

- The choice between the data immersion and the wall model is the same for all no-slip geometries. It would be useful to be able to choose per geometry, so that thin parts, which are better handled by the data immersion, can be combined with the wall model for the rest.
- The friction velocity is computed separately in the wall model of the velocity, from the log-law and the velocity at the reference point, and in the wall functions of the turbulence model, from k. In equilibrium these are the same, but they could be made consistent by sharing the value.
- The wall distance used in the turbulence wall functions is limited to at least half a cell, and the wall model always samples the velocity at a fixed distance from the wall. Both are simple choices that could be refined.
